Vendor Proposal Review

An assessment of the proposed roadmap and staffing model for the initial GPS release: what holds up, what does not, and what to ask before agreeing to anything.

00What was proposed

“Timeline & Phasing: The initial version is estimated to take 5 months. Month 1 will be dedicated to delivering a Proof of Concept (POC) with core display capabilities, followed by full-scale development.”

“Resource Requirements: The proposed team structure requires 9 members: 1 Solutions Architect, 5 Developers, 1 DevOps Engineer, and 2 QA Engineers. Recruiting experienced talent will be necessary to meet these technical requirements.”

“Quality Assurance Strategy: QA and testing will run concurrently with development rather than as a final phase to prevent bottlenecks and ensure an on-schedule release.”

Three sentences, one number, no scope statement, no dependencies, no assumptions, and no definition of what “the initial version” contains. That absence is the finding, not a formatting complaint — there is no way to tell whether the five months was derived from anything, and no way to tell whether they and you are describing the same system.

01Verdict

Timeline
Roughly right

Five months is a defensible number for this. It is very likely right for the wrong reasons.

Team size
~5× too large

45 person-months for work that needs about 9. Nothing in the design parallelises across five developers.

Scope coverage
Half the project absent

No hardware, no program numbers, no SIMs, no installation, no verification. The critical path is unmentioned.

The POC
Wrong risk

A display POC proves the thing nobody doubted and defers every question that could break the design.

Hiring
Understated

“Recruiting will be necessary” is a two-to-three month prefix to the five months, stated as an aside.

QA strategy
A truism

Concurrent QA is not a strategy. Nothing about test data, device simulation, or what is actually hard to test here.

The one sentence to take away

They have produced a software plan for a project whose critical path is physical. Everything else in this review follows from that. It is not a sign of a bad team — it is the standard shape of an estimate written by people who have built dashboards and have not yet had to wait three weeks for an auto-electrician and an idle excavator.

02Same calendar, five times the cost

This is the finding that matters commercially. Their five months and an in-house two-engineer plan land in roughly the same month — because after about week nine the constraint stops being software capacity. It becomes a distributor's stock, a vendor's response time, a carrier's account-opening process, and a machine that is out earning money and cannot be wired today.

Nine people cannot compress any of that. What they can do is build more software in parallel — and there is very little parallel software to build. The core of this system is one telemetry contract, one schema, one tenancy model and one ingestion path. Those are one-to-two-person artefacts by nature: split them across five developers and you get coordination overhead and divergent designs, not speed.

The fair version of this

Nine people is not an absurd number for a large scope. It is an absurd number for this scope. Which is why section 10's first question is the only one that really matters: what do they think they are building? If their answer includes a customer portal, mobile apps, a public API and a multi-region deployment, the estimate is reasonable and the misunderstanding is yours to fix. If their answer is “the GPS platform”, the estimate is five times too big.

03What is missing

Seven workstreams, all serial, all externally owned, none mentioned. Two of them decide whether the project can deliver its central capability at all:

  • CAN program numbers. Whether a given machine will yield ECU engine hours depends on its make, model and year having a supported program from the device vendor. Until that list is confirmed, nobody knows which machines can carry a utilisation record — and a plan that has not asked cannot know what it is committing to.
  • Install verification. A device that reports position but no engine hours looks perfectly healthy. If “installed” does not mean “engine hours observed on our server”, the failure is discovered months later, on a machine that has since moved site.

Also absent: any reference to the work that already exists. Seven dashboard screens are built as a working prototype, the device and protocol specification is written, and the architecture reference is current. A proposal that starts a display POC from zero has either not read them or is scoping greenfield — and either answer is worth knowing.

04The POC is the wrong POC

Month one on “core display capabilities” front-loads the least risky work in the project. Rendering a map and some tables is not in doubt; it is the part of the system with the most prior art and the fewest unknowns — and in this case a version of it already exists.

A proof of concept should attack the thing most likely to be wrong. Here that is a short list, and none of it is visual:

The real unknownProved byCosts
Do the devices behave as documented?One or two units on a bench, connecting to a listener, completing the handshake and acknowledgementTwo devices, a SIM, a few days
Does the CAN adapter yield engine hours on our machines?One adapter fitted to one real machine of a make we actually ownOne install, one electrician day
Does the ingestion path acknowledge only after a durable write?Killing the store mid-transmission and confirming the device replays with no gapAn afternoon
Does an outage replay reconstruct correctly?Disconnecting a device for hours, then checking trips and hour deltas after the backlog drainsA day, mostly waiting

What a display POC actually demonstrates

That the team can build a UI, against data someone has handed them in a convenient shape. Every hard question — protocol behaviour, ECU access, durability, replay, attribution — survives untouched into full-scale development, which is the worst place to discover any of them. A POC that never touches a real device is a demo.

06The hiring assumption

“Recruiting experienced talent will be necessary to meet these technical requirements” is doing an enormous amount of quiet work in that sentence. It means the five-month clock cannot start until the team exists.

StageRealistic
Sourcing and interviewing 9 people4–8 weeks
Notice periods4–12 weeks
Visas and onboarding, if hiring from outside the country4–8 weeks
A team that has never worked together becoming productive4–6 weeks

These overlap, but not by much, and the last one is the least discussed and most reliable. Five months of delivery realistically sits behind two to three months of assembly — call it seven to eight months from agreement to a working pilot, against a plan that says five.

It is also worth naming plainly what the sentence implies commercially: you are being asked to fund a team build. That may be exactly what you want, and it is a different decision from buying a delivery.

07“QA runs concurrently with development”

Nobody has proposed the alternative since about 2005. This is a statement everyone agrees with, which is what makes it unfalsifiable and therefore not a strategy. It tells you nothing about what will be tested or how.

The quality risks in this system are specific and unusual, and none of them is addressed by conventional test practice:

  • Out-of-order arrival. A device replaying a four-hour outage delivers records hours old, interleaved with live ones. Every calculation must produce the same answer regardless of arrival order.
  • Duplicate delivery. A slow acknowledgement causes the device to resend an identical packet minutes later on a different connection. Deduplication has to be on record identity, not delivery.
  • Unobserved gaps. A connectivity gap must be flagged as unknown, never inferred as an idle machine. Getting this wrong understates usage and nothing in the system looks broken.
  • Tenancy at a timestamp. A record resolving to the wrong tenant is the worst defect this platform can have, and it is invisible in every normal test.
  • Clock skew. Devices with implausible timestamps corrupt trips and hour deltas silently.

Ask what their test approach is for a four-hour outage replay. The answer distinguishes a team that has built telemetry from a team that has built web applications.

08Where they may be right

A review that finds nothing good is usually a review with an agenda. Three things in this proposal are sound:

  • Five months is honest. Plenty of vendors would have said eight weeks to win the work. Five months for a first release of a telemetry system is a realistic number, and it suggests they have some idea of the difficulty.
  • A dedicated architect is correct. This system has three or four decisions that are expensive to reverse — tenancy, the ingestion boundary, the evidence path — and they benefit from one person owning coherence.
  • Concurrent QA is directionally right, even if stated as a platitude. A final test phase on a system fed by real devices would be a genuine mistake.

And the scope caveat stands: if they believe they are building substantially more than the GPS layer, the team size may be defensible and the fault is a missing scope statement rather than a padded estimate. Establish that before challenging the number.

The sequencing change that matters more than the headcount

Raise the first purchase order and the program-number enquiry before the architecture is agreed. Nothing in the physical track depends on the software design being settled, and the default sequence — settle the design, then order hardware — adds six to eight weeks that no amount of staffing recovers.

10Questions to send back

In order. The first one changes the meaning of every other answer.

  1. What does “the initial version” contain? A written scope. Specifically: does it include hardware procurement, installation and commissioning, or only software?
  2. Which five workstreams are the five developers on, and what are the interfaces between them?
  3. Who confirms the CAN program numbers for our fleet, and when does that enquiry go out?
  4. Who owns procurement, SIM activation, electrician scheduling and install verification?
  5. What is the definition of a completed install? If the answer is not “engine hours observed on our server”, that is the conversation.
  6. What ingestion path are you assuming — adopting an existing server, or building a protocol terminator? What is the DevOps engineer deploying?
  7. How do you test a four-hour outage replay? And how do you generate device traffic before hardware exists?
  8. Does the 5 months start at contract signature or at team completion? What is the hiring plan, and who carries the risk if it takes longer?
  9. Have you read the existing architecture reference and seen the seven screens that already exist? What in the POC is not already built?
  10. What are the three assumptions that, if wrong, break the 5 months? An estimate whose author cannot answer this has not been stress-tested.

11How to run the conversation

  • Lead with scope, not with the number. Challenging headcount first invites a defensive re-quote. Establishing what they think they are building either resolves the gap or makes it undeniable.
  • Do not present the counter-estimate as a target. Present it as a question: here is what we think this takes and why — what do you see that we do not? A team that engages with that is worth working with; a team that restates the original number is telling you something.
  • Ask for the physical track in writing. Whoever ends up doing the work, that track must appear in the plan with owners and lead times. Its absence is the most reliable predictor of the date slipping.
  • Make the first month cheap and decisive. Two devices, one adapter, one machine, one listener. It costs almost nothing, answers the questions that could invalidate the design, and it tells you more about the team than any proposal document will.
  • Keep the telemetry contract. Whoever builds the ingestion tier, the record shape everything downstream consumes stays ours. Give that away and the ability to change vendor or approach goes with it.
