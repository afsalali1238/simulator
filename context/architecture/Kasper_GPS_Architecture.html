Kasper GPS Architecture

The GPS stack end to end: Teltonika FMC130 hardware on the machine, the ingestion path that terminates it, and the store that turns position and engine hours into a utilisation record which still holds up months later. Scoped to what comes off the machine and how far it travels; everything downstream of the API is out of scope.

00Scope and status

This covers one layer: how position and engine data gets off an excavator, into a store, and back out as a live map, an alert, and a utilisation record. Where this data is consumed by other systems, only the contract it is consumed through appears here. Analytics over accumulated history is a later piece of work and is deliberately not scoped here.

How to use this document

This is a reference, not a source of truth.

The primary sources are the Teltonika documentation and the device's own configuration, the SUDO and AWS threads, and decisions the team actually takes. Anything here marked Open is unresolved — treat it as a question to answer, not a position to build on. Where a number appears, it is an order-of-magnitude estimate for reasoning about shape, not a figure to plan or quote from.

Three labels are used throughout, and they mean different things. Reference is device or protocol behaviour taken from Teltonika's documentation — not ours to decide, but still worth verifying against the firmware we actually receive. Proposed is a position this document argues for, which needs the tech team's agreement before anything is built on it. Open is genuinely unresolved, with no position taken.

Nothing here is marked settled, because nothing here has been through the team yet. Read it, argue with it, and tell me where it is wrong — that is more useful than agreement.

01What the system does

Five capabilities, in dependency order. Each one is only as good as the one above it, which is why the document is ordered the way it is rather than by component.

CapabilityWhat it requiresPhase
LocateA device reporting position on a schedule, and a store that can answer "where is asset X now"Pilot
ReconstructHistory retained in time order and queryable by asset over a window — route playback, trips, stopsPilot
DetectRules evaluated against the stream, plus a scheduled sweep for the conditions defined by absencePilot
AttributeEvery record resolvable to one asset and one tenant at the record's own timestamp — section 09Pilot
EvidenceECU engine hours, durably acknowledged, immutably stored, and sealed into a period that can be reproduced later — section 12Pilot

Evidence is the one that constrains the design

Locate, reconstruct and detect are satisfied by any competent tracking stack. Attribute and evidence are not, and they are what the awkward parts of this architecture exist for: acknowledge-after-durability, an immutable cold tier, dated assignment windows, and a sealed utilisation period. Engine hours are used as contractual evidence for how long a machine ran on a site, so a record that is merely probably right is not useful — it has to be attributable and reproducible months later.

Everything else follows from that. If a proposal makes locate cheaper at the expense of attribute or evidence, it is solving the wrong problem.

Not every asset can do all five

An asset produces ECU engine hours only when the CAN adapter is fitted and its make, model and year have a supported program number. An FMC130 missing either one still delivers locate, reconstruct and detect — it simply stops short of evidence, and it stops short silently. Section 04 draws the decision; section 09 enforces it.

02The constraints that shape everything

Every non-obvious decision downstream traces to one of these. If a proposal conflicts with one, the proposal is wrong.

C1 · Engine hours are evidence

ECU-sourced hours are the input to the utilisation record for a hire period, and that record is relied on afterwards by people who were not there. The record path therefore has to be defensible, not merely available.

Forces: acknowledge-after-durability, an immutable cold tier, dated assignment history, and integrity enforced at ingress rather than only in storage.

C2 · Cellular bandwidth is the scarce resource

Every record crosses a metered mobile link. Reporting interval, record size and how aggressively the device batches are the levers that decide data volume — a 10-second interval moves roughly three times the data of a 30-second one, for the same fleet.

Forces: the interval is an explicit decision, not a default; adaptive reporting (dense while moving, sparse while stationary) is the obvious optimisation; and the pipeline should never require a round trip per record.

C3 · The device speaks binary TCP

Codec 8/8E over raw TCP with an IMEI handshake and a 4-byte record-count ACK. The session is stateful across the whole exchange.

Forces: a stateful socket server, adopted or written. No managed MQTT broker can sit at the edge, and no IoT service can terminate these devices directly.

C4 · Identity is an IMEI in cleartext

No client certificate, no cryptographic credential. Anyone who reaches the listener can present a valid IMEI and inject records we would then bill against.

Forces: network-layer control as the primary defence — private APN first, allow-listing second. A tamper-evident ledger downstream does not fix this.

C5 · Tenants are numerous and assets move between them

Tenants range from five assets to two hundred, and an excavator works for one tenant this quarter and another next. Tenancy is therefore a property of a time window, not of a row.

Forces: one shared deployment with row-level isolation; ownership as a dated assignment record, not a foreign key on the asset.

C6 · Ingest is bursty and out of order

Sites have patchy coverage — equipment moves behind structures, into basements, near interference. Devices buffer to flash and replay on reconnect.

Forces: idempotency on record identity not delivery, a store that accepts historical writes without penalty, and gaps flagged as unknown rather than inferred as idle — an accuracy control, not a UX nicety.

Reading the diagram

Two things it is worth being precise about. The processing column is not a service per box — those are seven consumers of one stream, and at pilot scale several of them are functions in the same process. 04Hardware

FMC130 + ALL-CAN300 is the device this design assumes

The FMC130 with an ALL-CAN300 on the CAN bus is what produces ECU engine hours, and engine hours are what the evidence capability rests on. The suggestion is to design, size and test against this combination. Note that it is a combination: the tracker alone gives position and ignition, and the adapter is what turns it into an equipment-monitoring device.

4.2 The CAN adapter and its per-model dependency

The ALL-CAN300 taps the machine's CAN H and CAN L pair, listens for the values we care about, and hands decoded data to the FMC130 over a separate two-wire serial link. It reads; it never controls. Two consequences the architecture has to carry:

  • What it can read depends on the exact make, model and year, and requires a Teltonika program number for that machine. This must be checked against the real fleet list before installs are scheduled — a mixed yard cannot be assumed uniform. An FMC130 on a machine with no supported program number reports position and ignition and nothing more — no engine hours, no fuel level, no fault codes, and therefore no utilisation record. The device is working; the machine simply will not tell it anything. This is invisible until the data arrives, which is why the fleet list has to be checked against Teltonika before installs are booked, not after.
  • CAN H and CAN L must never be swapped. Named here because it is the most common install error and it presents as "the device works but there is no engine data", which sends people looking in the software.

Commissioning sequence

SIM and backup battery while unpowered → bench pre-configuration (server address, protocol, APN, program number) → mount with sky view → power, ignition sense, ground → CAN adapter into the bus and into the tracker → power on and verify data arriving on our server before the install is called complete. The last step is the one that gets skipped, and it converts a five-minute fix into a second site visit.

05Protocol and connectivity

The device's behaviour is fixed whatever terminates the connection, so this section describes the protocol as the device presents it. Two of the properties below — rejecting an unknown IMEI at the handshake, and acknowledging only after a durable write — are things the server chooses to do. A purpose-built gateway would guarantee both by construction; on an adopted server neither is confirmed (D5). Read them as the target behaviour, not as an in-force guarantee of the pilot.

The session

1
DEVICE →Opens a TCP connection to the configured host and port.
2
DEVICE →Sends its IMEI: two length bytes 0x000F then 15 ASCII bytes.
3
← SERVERReplies 0x01 to accept or 0x00 to reject. The protocol offers an authorisation point here — an unknown or decommissioned IMEI can be refused before it transmits a single record. Whether an adopted server rejects at this moment or only after accepting data is D5, and it decides whether the baseline ingress control exists on path A at all.
4
DEVICE →Sends the AVL packet: preamble, data length, codec ID 0x08 or 0x8E, record count, records, repeated count, CRC-16/IBM.
5
SERVERValidates the CRC and that the two counts match. On mismatch: drop, no ACK, increment a metric. The device resends.
6
← SERVERReplies with a 4-byte record count. Setting ACK Type to AVL would make this acknowledgement an application decision we control rather than a network-layer one — TCP/IP mode would acknowledge on receipt, before anything is persisted, and remove the choice entirely. Setting AVL makes acknowledge-after-durability possible; the server has to actually do it, which a purpose-built gateway does by construction and an adopted server may not (D5).
7
DEVICEClears those records from flash. Holds the link for Open Link Timeout, then closes.

Never merge GNSS-estimated fuel with ECU fuel

AVL IDs 12 and 13 are GNSS-derived fuel estimates available on every device without CAN. ECU fuel arrives via adapter-specific CAN parameters. Separate columns, explicit source discriminator, all the way to the report. An estimate presented as a reading is a credibility loss that does not get recovered once noticed.

6.1 The reference implementation

Before it is a candidate to run, an existing open-source GPS server is a reference implementation — and the one to read is Traccar. Its value is not that we might deploy it. It is that it already does, in production, everything our ingestion tier would have to do — terminate the TCP connection, complete the IMEI handshake, validate CRC-16/IBM, parse Codec 8 and 8E, emit the acknowledgement, and keep a device registry. That makes it a working specification of the problem, and the honest benchmark for anything we build instead: if a proposed gateway does not do what it already does, it is not finished.

Use it that way first, whichever path is chosen. Read its protocol handling, run it against a real unit, and let it tell us what the devices actually do rather than what the documentation says they do. Whether an adopted server then stays in the architecture is a separate question, and the team's to answer.

A suggested starting point — to confirm, amend or reject

Path A for the pilot, on three technical grounds. Codec 8/8E parsing is the one part of this system already solved and field-tested by someone else, and reimplementing it buys nothing we can name. The pilot's real unknowns are physical — part numbers, CAN program numbers, wiring, SIM behaviour, buffer limits — and this path reaches real device traffic in days, which is when those unknowns start answering themselves. And a two-component system is one engineer can hold in their head while debugging a machine 40 kilometres away.

Path C once engine hours are relied on outside the team. Its justification is acknowledge-after-durability and an ingress control we own — real requirements, but ones that bite at the point where the figure leaves the team. If that point arrives before the pilot ends, the order above is wrong and C should come first.

Path B looks weakest, and this is the part of the suggestion to argue with hardest if you disagree. It needs the same custom bridge as C — the devices still cannot speak MQTT — and then routes the record through a broker we do not control, landing between A and C on acknowledgement control while carrying more moving parts than either. A managed IoT service earns its place when devices speak its protocol natively and you want its registry, shadow state and remote job execution; none of that applies to devices speaking Codec 8E over raw TCP whose firmware is managed by Teltonika's own FOTA service.

What would change this: a firmware release with native MQTT (which would make B credible and A unnecessary), a requirement for the evidence path from day one (which promotes C), or the adopted server turning out to acknowledge before persisting in a way we cannot patch (D5).

6.3 The rule that keeps this reversible

Nothing above L3 ever reads the terminator's own shape. A thin normaliser pulls from whatever terminates the socket and writes the Kasper telemetry contract; the dashboard, the rules engine and the ledger consume only that. If this rule holds, swapping that terminator is a change to one component. If it is broken even once — a dashboard screen reading the terminator's own JSON directly because it was quicker — the migration becomes a rewrite, and it will not happen.

07Processing

The telemetry contract

{
  "dedup_id":    "sha256(imei | ts_ms | payload_hash)",
  "device_id":   "uuid",             // our id, not the vendor's
  "imei":        "356307042441013",
  "asset_id":    "uuid | null",      // resolved by the enricher
  "ts_ms":       1755660000000,      // device time — NOT arrival time
  "received_ms": 1755660003411,      // the two differ on replay
  "gnss":        { "lat":…, "lon":…, "speed_kmh":22, "angle":…,
                   "sats":9, "accuracy_m":8 },
  "io":          { "ignition":1, "movement":1, "odometer_m":184203,
                   "ext_voltage":27.9 },
  "engine":      { "hours":4182.5, "rpm":1450, "fuel_level_pct":61 },
  "engine_source":"ecu",             // "ecu" | null — never inferred
  "fuel_source": "ecu",              // "ecu" | "gnss_estimate" | null
  "codec":       "8E"
}

An asset whose make, model and year have no supported CAN program number populates gnss and io but leaves engine null, and its fuel_source is gnss_estimate at best. Null means "not measured", and it must never be rendered as zero. An asset showing 0.0 engine hours because its adapter is unsupported looks identical to an asset that genuinely did not run — and only one of those can support a utilisation record.

Out-of-order arrival is normal traffic, not an error path

After a four-hour outage a device replays thousands of records with timestamps hours old, interleaved with live ones. Trip building, engine-hour accumulation and geofence evaluation must all be functions of ts_ms and must produce the same answer regardless of arrival order. A consumer that assumes monotonic time will be quietly wrong for exactly the periods a dispute asks about.

And do not alert from stale records. A geofence exit reconstructed from a six-hour-old record must not reach anyone's phone. Rules check received_ms − ts_ms against a staleness threshold and route stale evaluations to the log.

Idempotency is on record identity, not delivery

A device that does not get its ACK in time resends the identical packet, possibly minutes later on a different connection. dedup_id is computed from device-side content only — never from arrival time or connection — so every copy collapses to one. Each consumer must be idempotent on it independently, because consumers replay separately.

Start with one database

At pilot scale the hot/warm/relational split is three tables in one Postgres, with the warm one a TimescaleDB hypertable. That is not a compromise — TimescaleDB is Postgres, it handles late-arriving writes without penalty (which C6 requires), and collapsing three stores into one removes most of the operational surface at the stage where there is one operator. Split them out when a query pattern actually fights another, not on principle.

The exception is cold storage, which is separate from day one. It is the evidence tier, its whole value is that it cannot be modified by the same credentials that run the application, and retrofitting immutability onto records already written is not possible.

Batch the warm writes

Accumulate records per asset over a short window before persisting — one row per asset per minute holding six 10-second records. It cuts write volume roughly 6×, keeps per-write metering questions off the critical path, and lets the underlying store change later without touching consumers. Build it regardless of which store wins.

09Data model and tenancy

Pooled multi-tenancy, row-level isolation, cost scaling with devices rather than customers (C5). A fully isolated deployment remains possible for an operator that contractually requires it, but it is an exception to be justified case by case, not the default shape.

Two independently dated relationships

devices            (device_id PK, imei, model, firmware, iccid,
                    status, commissioned_at,
                    can_adapter bool)     -- fitted and verified at commissioning
assets             (asset_id PK, type, make, model, year, serial,
                    owner_vendor_id,
                    can_program_no NULL)  -- null = machine exposes no ECU data

device_assignments   -- which box is on which machine
  (device_id, asset_id, from_ts, to_ts NULL, installed_by, notes)

asset_assignments    -- whose machine it is, and when
  (asset_id, tenant_id, project_id, site_id, from_ts, to_ts NULL,
   contract_ref, basis)  -- 'utilisation' requires ECU capability

A device moves to a different machine when it fails or the machine is sold. A machine changes hands every time it is re-hired. Different schedules, so telemetry resolves through both windows to answer "whose engine hours are these, on which asset".

Capability is the pair, not the device

An asset produces ECU engine hours only when the assigned device has can_adapter true and the asset has a supported can_program_no. Neither field alone is sufficient, and neither is a property of the other — one is set when an electrician finishes an install, the other when Teltonika confirms a program for that make, model and year. Derive the capability from the pair and enforce it: an assignment with basis = 'utilisation' should not be openable on an asset that cannot source ECU hours. A constraint here costs an hour; finding out later means going back to the machine.

-- a tenant sees a record only if the asset was theirs when it was produced
SELECT t.*
FROM   telemetry t
JOIN   device_assignments da ON da.device_id = t.device_id
       AND t.ts >= da.from_ts AND t.ts < COALESCE(da.to_ts, 'infinity')
JOIN   asset_assignments  aa ON aa.asset_id  = da.asset_id
       AND t.ts >= aa.from_ts AND t.ts < COALESCE(aa.to_ts, 'infinity')
WHERE  aa.tenant_id = :caller_tenant;

Authorisation never reads the stamped tenant_id

A denormalised tenant_id on telemetry is fine as a query hint. It cannot be the basis of a permission decision, because records arrive late and assignments get corrected retrospectively when paperwork catches up with the yard — so a replayed record can carry a stamp that was simply wrong. Every permission decision resolves through the assignment window at ts. Get this wrong and one client sees another's utilisation, which is the worst failure this platform can have.

Roles

RoleSees
Client masterAll assets on hire to them, across projects and sites, for the periods that were theirs
Client site userOnly assets on their assigned sites
VendorOnly assets they own — across every tenant that has held them, and for the whole period, not just the current one.
Kasper opsEverything, for operations
Kasper adminEverything, with every cross-tenant access written to the audit log

Enforce as Postgres row-level security, not application WHERE clauses. RLS fails closed; a forgotten clause on a new endpoint fails open.

Note the asymmetry between vendor and client scope. A vendor sees their asset's whole life; a client sees only their hire window. Both read the same table through different policies, and the vendor policy is the wider one — worth stating because it is counter-intuitive and easy to implement backwards.

The utilisation period

utilisation_periods
  (period_id PK, asset_id, asset_assignment_id, contract_ref,
   start_ts, end_ts,
   engine_hours_start, engine_hours_end, engine_hours_delta,
   idle_hours, distance_km, gap_minutes_unknown,
   source,             -- 'ecu' only; a period without an ECU source is not evidence
   evidence_ref,       -- immutable object version + SHA-256
   sealed_at, sealed_by)

gap_minutes_unknown is not decoration. It is the explicit record of time the system could not observe, and it appears on the certificate. Understating usage because a device was offline is a trust problem far more expensive than the disclosure.

Once a hire period closes and the certificate is issued, the row is sealed and immutable. Corrections are new rows referencing the old one, never updates.

Surfaces beyond the internal dashboard

  • Client tracking page — one asset or one job, live position and verified arrival. Reads the same contract as the internal dashboard through a client-scoped policy, so it is a permission scope rather than a separate build. The link is a live location feed: signed, expiring, single-job tokens, revoked when the job closes.
  • Owner-scoped view — an asset owner seeing only their own assets, across every tenant that has held them. Architecturally this is a permission scope over the screens above rather than a new surface: same contract, same queries, different predicate (section 09). Whenever it ships, the predicate should exist and be tested from the start — retrofitting a scope is how cross-tenant leaks happen.
  • Utilisation certificate — the document the whole stack exists to produce. Renders from the sealed period, so a reissue six months later is byte-identical.

API shape

One internal telemetry API over the contract, permission-filtered by the section 09 predicate: current state per asset or tenant, history for an asset over a window, trips, utilisation for a period, alerts, device health. Public customer API and webhooks are P2 — but design the internal one as though it will be published, because it will be.

Delivery rules

  • One row per fired alert; one row per channel attempt with provider message ID and status. Retries are per attempt — a WhatsApp failure must never re-send the email.
  • Suppress before delivering. A device replaying an outage backlog can regenerate hundreds of historical crossings. Deduplicate on rule + asset + event window, and drop anything past the staleness threshold.
  • WhatsApp template approval has lead time. Draft and submit during build, not at pilot launch.
  • Carry acknowledged_at and acknowledged_by from day one even though acknowledgement is P2. Adding columns is easy; backfilling meaning is not.

12Security and evidence integrity

12.1 Device authentication — the honest position

Codec 8 identifies a device by IMEI, in cleartext, with no client certificate. Anyone who can reach the listener can present a valid IMEI and inject records we would then bill against. This cannot be solved downstream — a perfect tamper-evident ledger of unauthenticated inputs proves only that we faithfully recorded whatever someone sent. The control sits at ingress.

ControlStrengthAssessment
Private APN into our networkStrongestThe listener is never publicly reachable — removes the surface rather than defending it. Feasibility with Etisalat/e& and du at pilot scale is open and is a carrier question, not an AWS one. Pursue directly.
Carrier IP allow-listGoodCuts the internet down to the carrier's NAT ranges. Ranges change; needs a maintenance owner.
IMEI allow-list at handshakeBaselineBlocks unknown devices, not spoofed known ones. Necessary, not sufficient. An adopted server will have a device registry — Traccar does — but whether it rejects at the handshake or after accepting data is unverified (D5), and the answer decides whether this control exists on path A at all.
IMEI-to-source-IP bindingModerateDetection signal rather than a gate — carrier NAT makes it noisy.
TLS 1.2Transport onlyFrom firmware 03.27.02, server-authenticated. Whether the device offers a client certificate is open with Teltonika.

Recommendation: pilot on carrier allow-listing plus IMEI allow-list plus TLS; pursue private APN as the target state. Record which controls were in force for each period — if a dispute turns on data integrity, we need to state what applied at the time.

12.2 Shared tracking links are credentials

A client tracking link is a live location feed for whoever holds it. Signed, expiring, single-job tokens; revoked when the job closes. An unsigned or permanent link is a standing exposure of the tenant's site activity and the owner's asset movements to anyone the link reaches.

12.3 The evidence chain

1
INGRESSRecords accepted only from an allow-listed IMEI over TLS from an expected range, rejected at the handshake. Controls actually in force for each period are logged — which matters because on path A this step is weaker than described until D5 is answered.
2
DURABILITYNothing acknowledged until durably stored. A gap in our data is a device-buffer replay, never a silent loss.
3
IMMUTABILITYRaw and decoded records land in object storage under a retention lock. Not alterable by anyone, including us.
4
SEALINGA daily manifest of per-object hashes, record counts and the previous day's manifest hash — a hash chain, signed. A retrospective edit breaks the chain from that day forward.
5
TIME ANCHORSigning establishes integrity and origin, not time. A trusted time anchor on the manifest is what lets us say the record predates the dispute. Open.
6
ATTRIBUTIONThe utilisation period resolves through both dated windows — one client, one contract, one period.
7
SEALThe period is sealed with a pointer to a specific immutable object version. Corrections are new rows.

Note honestly: steps 1 and 2 are weaker under an adopted server than under a purpose-built gateway. That is the cost of path A as proposed, and it is arguable precisely while this is a monitoring capability rather than an evidence one — if the team disagrees about when that line is crossed, the ordering in section 06 changes. The trigger to migrate is not a device count — it is the first utilisation record issued from this data to anyone outside the team.

12.4 Other controls

  • Row-level security as the tenancy boundary; admin cross-tenant access always audited.
  • Encryption at rest on every store; TLS on every hop including internal.
  • Separate signing key for evidence, with usage restricted to the sealing service.
  • Immobiliser stays excluded. Technically a digital-output write; commercially a liability. Before it is ever considered: legal review, a hard interlock preventing activation while an asset is in motion, two-person authorisation, and its own audit trail. Not "just another command".

13Sizing

Traffic model

Per instrumented asset: roughly 9 hours ignition-on per day at one record every 10 seconds, plus low-rate heartbeats when off, plus event records. About 3,500 records per device per day. At 30-second reporting it is closer to 1,300 — the reason the interval decision matters.

The burst case

Not steady state — a carrier or site outage of several hours followed by mass reconnection. At 1,000 devices with a four-hour gap, roughly 1.4 million buffered records drain on reconnect. Two mitigations are already in the design: the device backoff schedule spreads reconnection rather than synchronising it, and the flash buffer means slow draining costs time, not data. Size to drain a four-hour backlog inside about thirty minutes and let backpressure hold the rest on the devices.

One socket server covers the whole realistic range

At 1,000 devices this is 40 records per second — a workload a single modest VM handles without difficulty, which is why the terminator is not where the scaling problem lives. The scaling pressure at these volumes is on the database and the dashboard's query patterns, not the socket server. Plan capacity there first; nobody has ever been surprised by a socket server at 40 rps, and plenty of people have been surprised by an unindexed range query over a telemetry table.

15Rollout and cutover

15.1 Hosting

Three answers currently exist across our documents: a self-hosted droplet, AWS me-south-1 (Bahrain), and AWS me-central-1 (UAE). Decide once and correct the others.

OptionForAgainst
DigitalOceanCheapest by a wide margin; simplest; matches path ANo UAE region; data residency story is weak for government and port clients
AWS me-central-1In-country data residency, which some clients require contractuallyNewer region — confirm every service the design needs is actually available
AWS me-south-1Older, more complete service coverageBahrain, not UAE. Answers the latency question nobody asked and loses the residency one that will be asked.

Recommendation: confirm feature availability in me-central-1 and default there, accepting a higher cost than DigitalOcean for a residency answer that closes enterprise deals. Fall back to me-south-1 only if something the design genuinely needs is absent. Whichever is chosen, reach the ingestion endpoint by a stable domain name, never a raw IP — an IP change means reconfiguring every device in the field, some of them bolted inside a machine on a site.

15.2 Parallel run

Teltonika's second-server setting in Duplicate mode sends every record to both servers and clears device storage only when both confirm. That gives a genuinely lossless migration between ingestion paths, in either direction, whenever the team decides to move.

1
SHADOWNew path configured as second server. Receives real traffic, acknowledges, drives nothing customer-facing.
2
RECONCILEDaily comparison of record counts, engine-hour deltas and trip boundaries. Divergence is a bug in the new path until proven otherwise. Run for a full reporting cycle, not a week.
3
CUTOVERSurfaces read from the new path; the old one keeps ingesting as the safety net.
4
RETIRESwap server order, then decommission.

The Duplicate-mode trap

Because records clear only when both servers confirm, the slower or less available endpoint governs every device's buffer. During parallel run an outage on either side fills flash across the whole fleet. Monitor buffer depth on both, and have a one-step rollback to single-server ready before starting.

15.3 Pilot rollout

Start with roughly 10 FMC130 units and their CAN adapters, on machines rather than trucks. Pilot on machines that go out on hire, because engine hours and the utilisation record are the thing being proven. Instrumenting whatever is nearest exercises locate and detect and never touches the evidence path at all.

The sequence that matters is not the software one: confirm CAN program numbers against the real fleet list → pick the pilot machines from the assets that have one → order FMC130s and adapters → activate SIMs → book the auto-electrician → install and verify engine hours arriving on our server. Every one of those has a lead time, and none is unblocked by writing more code. Run the software build in parallel against a traffic generator; the Codec 8/8E frame format is fully specified, so valid test packets can be constructed without hardware.

17Timeline and phasing

A preliminary shape for the initial release, offered so the team has something concrete to correct. Durations are ranges in weeks relative to a start, not calendar dates, and they assume the open items in section 18 are answered rather than waited on.

17.1 Two tracks, and only one of them is engineering

The instinct is to schedule this as a software project with a hardware step at the end. That gets the dependency backwards. The field track has the external lead times on it — a vendor answering a question, a distributor shipping, a carrier activating SIMs, a licensed electrician having a free day — and none of those shorten because the software is ready early. Start both at week zero.

The useful consequence: once devices are installed and reporting, the remaining software phases develop against real traffic rather than a generator. That is worth more than it sounds. Almost every surprise in a telematics build comes from what the devices actually send, not from what the pipeline does with it.

17.2 What the shape adds up to

Roughly 13–17 weeks of build, plus four weeks of observation — call it four to five months to a validated pilot, with about a month of that being elapsed time nobody can compress. P6 sits outside the initial release deliberately: hardening against traffic patterns that have not been measured yet is guesswork.

What one engineer alone looks like

Sequential rather than parallel, so the build stretches to roughly seven to nine months — and the failure mode is not slowness. It is that the field track quietly stops, because the same person is deep in a parser when the distributor needs a purchase decision and the electrician needs a date. If only one engineer is available, the coordinator role still has to belong to someone else, even if that someone is not technical. It is the cheapest schedule insurance available here.

Everything above can be argued with except the shape of P5. A pilot that reports "it worked" after a week has demonstrated that devices transmit — which was never in doubt. The full cycle is what surfaces the failures that matter: a machine that goes quiet for a weekend, an assignment that changes mid-period, a gap that has to be disclosed rather than smoothed over. Compressing that is the one shortcut that costs more than it saves.

TermMeaning
