Kasper GPS Delivery Plan

A preliminary roadmap and staffing model for the initial release: what has to happen, in what order, who is needed when, and which dependencies actually set the date.

00Scope and assumptions

The initial release is taken to mean: a pilot fleet instrumented and verified, the seven Fleet screens running on live device data, a utilisation record produced from ECU engine hours, and a reconciled month behind it. Not a public launch, not a customer-facing rollout — the first point at which the system can be trusted to say something true about a machine.

How to read the numbers

Weeks are relative, not calendar dates. Week 1 is the day the first purchase order is raised. Nothing here is committed to a date because two of the largest inputs — vendor response time and machine availability — are not ours to schedule.

Durations are estimates for reasoning about sequence and shape. They assume a team that has not built this before, which is the honest assumption, and they include no holiday, no competing priorities and no second project. Treat the ordering as the useful part and the durations as a starting position to argue with.

Assumed

  • Pilot size around ten machines, chosen from assets that go out on hire.
  • Hardware is FMC130 with the CAN adapter — the combination the architecture assumes.
  • The build is in-house. If an external partner takes the ingestion tier, section 08 sets out what changes.
  • Machines are working assets. They are instrumented when they are idle, not when the schedule would prefer.
  • No existing M2M account with the carrier. If one exists, take two weeks off the physical track.

01The shape of the problem

There are two tracks, and only one of them is under the team's control.

The software track

Contract, ingestion, data model, surfaces, ledger. Estimable, compressible with more people at some phases, and recoverable when it slips — a bad week can be made up.

It is also the track everyone instinctively plans first, because it is the one they can see.

The physical track

Program numbers from Teltonika, devices from a distributor, SIMs from a carrier, an electrician, and a machine standing still long enough to be wired.

Not estimable, not compressible, not recoverable. Every wait belongs to someone else's calendar, and the steps are strictly serial.

The single most important scheduling decision

Start the physical track in week one, before the software design is settled. Nothing in it depends on the architecture being finished — the fleet list, the program-number enquiry, the carrier account and one or two test units can all be in motion while the ingestion path is still being argued about.

The default behaviour is the opposite: settle the design, then order hardware. That sequence adds six to eight weeks to the end date and buys nothing, because the design questions that hardware would answer stay open the whole time.

Order one or two test units immediately

Ahead of the fleet order, ahead of the program-number answer, ahead of everything. Two units on a bench are worth more than any amount of documentation: they answer the open protocol questions directly, they let P1 onward be written against real Codec 8E traffic rather than a generator, and they turn the largest technical unknown in the architecture — how an adopted server actually behaves at the handshake and the acknowledgement — into something observable in week four rather than week ten.

03The critical path

Two consequences worth stating plainly. The end date is set by the fleet list, because everything downstream waits on knowing which machines have a supported program number — and that list has to be accurate about make, model and year, not approximate. And the last link is the longest. Installation is two to three weeks not because wiring a device takes long, but because ten machines are rarely idle at the same time, on the same site, on days the electrician is free.

04Phase definitions

PhaseWeeksDeliversCarries the risk of
P0 · Contract1–2.5The telemetry contract, the normaliser skeleton, and a traffic generator producing valid Codec 8E framesNothing visible. This is the phase most likely to be skipped and the one that makes every later phase a contained change.
P1 · Ingestion2.5–6Terminator stood up, device registry, normaliser writing the contract into a store, immutable archiveThe protocol assumptions. If the devices behave differently from the documentation, it surfaces here.
P2 · Model6–9Assets, devices, both assignment tables, row-level security, enricher, state projectorTenancy. Expensive to retrofit and the failure is silent.
P3 · Surfaces9–13Seven Fleet screens on live data, geofences and rules, message deliveryScope. This is where features get invented, and it overlaps the install window.
P4 · Ledger13–16Utilisation ledger, periods, gap accounting, sealing, record renderingCorrectness under replay and out-of-order arrival. Hard to test without months of messy real data.
P5 · Pilot16–20Pilot running, reconciliation against the existing path, operational runbookReality. Everything the first four phases assumed gets tested by machines on sites.

P0 and P1 carry the technical risk; P2 carries the design risk; P5 carries the risk nobody can estimate. If time has to come out, it comes out of P3 — a screen deferred is recoverable, a tenancy model retrofitted is not.

05Gates

What has to be demonstrably true to leave each phase. Not a status update — a thing someone can watch happen.

LeavingOnly when
P0Generated frames decode into the contract with sources set correctly, and no surface references an ingestion-specific shape
P1A real test unit's data reaches the store and the archive; killing the store mid-flight causes the device to replay with no gap
P2A record resolves to exactly one tenant at its own timestamp, and a cross-tenant query returns nothing under row-level security
P3A real geofence crossing on a real machine reaches a real phone, and a site-scoped user sees only their own assets
P4A hire period produces a sealed record that resolves to an immutable object version and discloses its unobserved gaps
P5A full reporting cycle reconciles against the existing path with every difference explained, not merely small

One gate is different from the others

P1's gate asks you to break the system on purpose — kill the store while a device is mid-transmission and confirm the device replays rather than losing the record. It is the only gate that tests the property the whole architecture is built around, and it takes an afternoon. Skipping it means finding out during a dispute instead.

06Staffing model

Peak is 2.3 FTE at P3; the twenty-week release costs roughly 36 person-weeks in total. Both numbers assume the same people stay on it throughout — a plan that hands the work between people at phase boundaries costs materially more, because the tenancy model and the telemetry contract are the kind of thing that has to be held in one head to stay coherent.

The role most often left unassigned

Field and procurement is a real job, not admin someone absorbs. Seven person-weeks of chasing a distributor, getting a carrier account opened, extracting program numbers from a vendor, and finding ten machines standing still. When it is nobody's named responsibility it defaults to whoever is busiest, and it is the track that sets the end date. Name a person.

08Staffing scenarios

ScenarioWeeksWhat actually changes
One engineer~24P3 stretches by about four weeks because screens follow the ledger rather than running alongside it. Everything else is unchanged — P0 to P2 were never going to parallelise.
Two engineers assumed~20The plan above. The second engineer earns their place in P3 and is under-used in P0–P2; the honest use of them early is the traffic generator, the install verification tooling, and taking the field-and-procurement role.
Three engineers~19Almost no gain. The physical track becomes the binding constraint before the software does, and the third engineer spends P0–P2 waiting on decisions only one person can make.
Partner builds ingestion~20Not faster, and differently risky. Removes P0–P1 from the internal team and adds a specification, a review cycle and an integration seam. Worth it to buy capacity, not to buy time — and the telemetry contract must stay ours or the reversibility rule is lost on day one.

Why adding people barely moves it

The software is not the long pole after roughly week nine. Below three engineers the constraint is the backend; at three and above it is a distributor, a vendor and a machine that is out earning. Beyond the second engineer, money is better spent shortening the physical track — paying for expedited shipping, a second electrician, or simply starting four weeks earlier — than on more software capacity.

11Out of scope for the initial release

Named so that leaving them out is a decision rather than an oversight, and so nobody plans around them being present.

  • Analytics over accumulated history. Separate work, and it needs history that will not exist for months.
  • The purpose-built ingestion gateway. The plan assumes an adopted server. Building the gateway instead adds roughly four to six weeks to P1 and is worth it only if engine hours are relied on outside the team from day one.
  • Owner-scoped external access. The predicate should exist and be tested; the surface can follow.
  • Scheduled report delivery, acknowledgement and escalation, public API and webhooks. Schema should carry the fields; behaviour can wait.
  • Anything requiring a second device type or a second protocol.
