00How to read this

This handbook answers a single practical question: what does it actually take to build Dozr's GPS and telematics platform to production grade, and who should build it? It is the resourcing-and-delivery companion to two other documents. The PRD says what the system must do; the repo's CLAUDE.md encodes the rules any builder (human or AI) must never break; and the earlier independent build review argued the technical positions this handbook now turns into a plan. Where those overlap, this document defers to them and points rather than repeats.

One boundary worth stating plainly: this is only the device-data layer — the backend that turns Teltonika hardware into trustworthy engine-hours, positions, and evidence, plus the API that feeds the fleet dashboard. The dashboard itself (fleet-v2/, nine screens) is already built on mock data. The Marketplace and Vendor OS are out of scope here entirely.

01What we're building

Dozr already has a telematics dashboard. What it does not have is anything behind it. fleet-v2/ renders nine screens — Fleet Map, Fuel, Maintenance, Geofences, Utilisation, Cost & ROI, Reports, Timesheet, Alerts Center — entirely from mock data. This project builds the real thing underneath: the layer that takes bytes off a Teltonika device on an excavator and turns them into a number you can put on an invoice and defend.

The cleanest way to hold the whole system in your head is as a chain of five capabilities, each depending on the one before it. This is the spine the PRD is organised around, and every module in section 05 slots onto it.

1LOCATEReceive each device's transmission without loss and know where every asset is now. The socket server, the protocol handshake, the acknowledgement discipline.
2RECONSTRUCTStore history so any asset's past — routes, stops, states — can be replayed exactly. Time-series storage, trip building.
3DETECTTurn raw signals into events: geofence entry/exit, idling, movement, fault codes, tampering. Rules and enrichment.
4ATTRIBUTETie every record to the right asset and the right customer at the moment it happened — dated assignment windows, multi-tenant isolation.
5EVIDENCEProduce engine-hours a customer will be billed on, sealed so the figure can be trusted and audited months later. The utilisation ledger and evidence chain.

The reason this ordering matters — and the reason the project is harder than "put dots on a map" — is that the money is at the far end. Dozr's GPS-as-a-Service sells utilisation billing: a customer pays for the hours a hired machine actually ran, read from its engine ECU. That single fact drives almost every hard decision in this handbook. It is why acknowledgement-after-durability is non-negotiable, why an estimated engine-hour is worthless where an ECU-read one is gold, and why the least glamorous modules (the ledger, the evidence seal) are the ones that most need a careful hand.

The one-sentence version: you are building a small, reliable pipe from a construction machine's ECU to a billing-grade number — and the engineering difficulty lives in trust and correctness, not in volume or fancy features. Get five capabilities right in order and the dashboard you already have lights up with real data.

03Hardware & field resources

This is the half of the project a pure software plan forgets, and it is the half that sets your earliest possible date. None of it is exotic, but all of it has a lead time measured in someone else's calendar.

Devices & adapters

PartForReads engine data via
Teltonika FMC130Heavy equipment — excavators, cranes, loaders, backhoes, generatorsALL-CAN300 adapter (construction/ag CAN)
Teltonika FMC920Light & support vehicles — pickups, trucksLV-CAN200 adapter (light-vehicle CAN)

Your fleet spans nine asset types (trailers, tippers, cranes, excavators, generators, boom lifts / loaders, backhoes, forklifts, plus support vehicles). The device reads position and ignition on almost anything; it reads engine hours only when the machine's make/model/year has a supported CAN program and the machine actually broadcasts the value. That gap is the whole of decision D1 and is covered below.

The gating item: CAN program numbers (D1)

Teltonika decodes CAN data using a per-make/model/year "program number." A machine that isn't on the supported list yields position and ignition only — no engine hours, which means no utilisation billing for that unit. You cannot know which of your real machines can be billed until you send your fleet list to Teltonika and get the programs back. This is the single most schedule-critical piece of paper in the project, and it is a field/procurement task, not a software one. Send it first.

The test bench — order two units now

Before any installs, one heavy rig (FMC130 + ALL-CAN300) and one light rig (FMC920 + LV-CAN200) with live SIMs, sitting on a desk, lets the software track begin against real device traffic instead of only a simulator. Confirm the firmware shipping is 03.27.02 or later — TLS support starts there, and older units need a firmware pass you'd rather not discover mid-pilot D9.

Define "installed" as "verified," not "fitted." A device can be wired in, powered, and reporting a healthy position while producing no engine data at all — and it looks perfectly fine on a map. An installation is only complete when engine hours for that machine have been seen arriving on the server. Make that the electrician's sign-off criterion and the coordinator's checklist item, or you will discover dead CAN links one invoice cycle too late.

Code you don't have to write

Three things are worth reusing rather than building. Traccar itself is the big one — even if you eventually replace it, its device-protocol handling is a working reference implementation of exactly the handshake and acknowledgement behaviour you must get right. The open Codec 8E parsers on GitHub save you from decoding the binary format from the spec by hand. And for engine hours specifically, the ISO 15143-3 (AEMP 2.0) standard and OEM telematics feeds — Cat Product Link, Komatsu KOMTRAX, Volvo CareTrack, John Deere JDLink — give you manufacturer-authoritative engine hours for machines that have them, with no CAN wiring at all. On a mixed fleet, the best answer is often "OEM feed where it exists, Teltonika CAN where it doesn't."

Licence conditions that actually matter for a paid product

Most of the stack is permissive (Apache/BSD/MIT) — use freely. Three carry conditions: PostGIS (GPL-2.0) and Grafana (AGPL-3.0) are safe as long as you run them unmodified behind your service and don't redistribute modified copies; standard SaaS use does not trigger their copyleft. TimescaleDB's advanced features sit under a source-available licence that only forbids reselling it as a database service — irrelevant to you. None of these block a commercial Dozr product; they only mean "don't fork-and-resell the tool itself." When in doubt on a specific repo (especially the community parsers), read its LICENSE file before shipping.

Environments & hosting

Three environments — local (Docker Compose on a laptop), staging (one small cloud VM), production (the same, hardened) — is enough through pilot. The one decision with weight is region D3: a DigitalOcean droplet is cheapest, but if UAE data-residency becomes a sales requirement, AWS me-central-1 (UAE) or me-south-1 (Bahrain) is the answer, at higher cost. Decide it before you accept any hosting estimate, because it changes every one of them.

05Modules & who owns them

The system breaks into nine modules. Read the "primary owner" column as a role, not a headcount — in the recommended team one person wears several of these hats. What matters is that each module has a single accountable owner and that the hard part is understood before work starts, because in this system the hard parts are correctness traps, not size problems.

The spine is modules 1 → 2 → 3 → 5. Ingestion feeds decode feeds the model feeds the ledger, and each depends on the one before being correct. That's why this is a small-team job: the core is a single, careful line of work that does not parallelise cleanly across many people. Modules 6 (surfaces) and 9 (field ops) do run in parallel once the record schema is frozen and the devices arrive — those are your two genuine parallel tracks.

The one role you cannot make part-time

The backend engineer owns the spine — ingestion, decode, model, ledger — and those modules must be held in one head as a coherent whole, because a mistake in any of them silently corrupts the billing number at the end. This is the seat to hire well and protect from context-switching. Everything else can flex around it. If budget forces a single hire, it is this one; the frontend, infra, and even some lead work can be part-time, deferred, or Claude-assisted (see 09).

Notice what is not on this list: no data-science role (the analytics here is arithmetic and rules, not modelling), no dedicated QA head at pilot scale (the backend engineer and lead own correctness), and no separate mobile team (delivery is WhatsApp, not a native app). Adding those is how a two-person job becomes a nine-person one.

07Team shape & how to run it

The recommendation is a two-engineer team plus a part-time field coordinator — one backend full-time, one frontend part-time, the coordinator running procurement and installs in parallel — with the lead/architect hat worn by afzl or the backend engineer. That is roughly 33 person-weeks of work, or about four to five calendar months to a validated pilot once hardware arrives.

Why nine people would be slower, not faster

The instinct that a bigger team ships sooner is wrong for this system, because the core is serial and correctness-critical. Ingestion, decode, model, and ledger have to be built in order and held consistent — you cannot put four engineers on one socket and one schema without them spending more time coordinating than building. A nine-person team here mostly generates meetings, merge conflicts, and diffusion of the one thing that matters (who is accountable for the billing number). The work is ~9 person-months; spread over the right two people it lands in ~4–5 calendar months. Spread over nine, it takes longer and costs five times as much.

The seam: what runs in series, what runs in parallel

Day 1D1 goes out immediately. The CAN-program request to Teltonika runs in the background for the whole project — nothing hardware-related can be finalised until it returns, so it must not wait for anything.
SerialThe spine — ingestion → decode → model → ledger. One line of work, mostly one engineer, each stage validated before the next leans on it.
Parallel ASurfaces. Once the canonical record schema is frozen, the frontend wires fleet-v2 against that contract (mocked at first, live as the spine delivers) without blocking on it.
Parallel BField ops. Once devices and CAN programs arrive, procurement, install, and verification proceed alongside the software — a different person, a different calendar.

How to run it: thin slice first, then widen

The single most useful piece of process advice from the review: buy the first six weeks, not the five months. Instead of a month-one display prototype that proves nothing risky, spend the opening weeks driving one thin vertical slice all the way through — a single real device, sending real CAN data, terminating on your server, stored durably, surfacing engine hours on one real screen. That slice touches every hard part once and retires the biggest risks (D1, the ACK discipline, engine-hours decoding) before you scale the width. Everything after is widening a proven pipe, which is the easy part.

Run it on a weekly demo cadence: every week ends with something observable working, not a status update. Use phase gates (section 08) that are defined by a proof — "a record survived a server restart," "engine hours matched the machine's own meter" — never by a calendar date alone. And keep the four documents in their lanes: this handbook is the who and how, the PRD is the what, CLAUDE.md is the rules a coding agent must obey, and the expert review is the why behind the positions. When they disagree, the PRD wins on scope and CLAUDE.md wins on invariants.

09What Claude can build — and where you need a human

This is the heart of your question. The short answer: Claude can carry a large share of the software, a human must own review on all of it, and two areas are irreducibly human — the billing-evidence correctness call and everything physical. The failure mode of this system is a silently wrong number, and no coding agent can self-certify that its output is billing-grade. That single fact sets the whole split below.

The honest bottom line: Claude can lead modules 3, 4, 6, and 7 outright and materially assist on 1, 2, and 8 — realistically well over half of the software, and the parts that are tedious rather than dangerous. What Claude cannot do is own the billing-evidence correctness call (module 5) or anything physical (module 9), and it cannot be the last set of eyes on its own work anywhere. So the shape that works is Claude for breadth and speed, one experienced human owning the spine's durability and the evidence chain, and a human review gate on every module.

10Build it yourself, or hand it to the dev team?

You asked what the issue is with building it yourselves rather than giving it to the development team. Here are the three honest paths, then a recommendation — because "undecided, help me choose" is exactly the right place to be before committing real money.

PathWhat it isUpsideThe real risk
A · Self-build with Claudeafzl + Claude, pulling in a specialist only for the hard partsCheapest; full control; you keep the billing-integrity knowledge in-house — your actual moatThe correctness-critical modules (ingestion durability, the evidence ledger) need real expertise; slow if afzl is effectively solo
B · Small teamThe 2-engineer + part-time-coordinator team in this handbookRight-sized; builds the capability in-house; moderate cost (raise ~AED 110–135k)Everything rides on hiring one backend engineer who genuinely understands device durability and billing evidence — that hire is the bottleneck
C · Hand to a dev teamThe nine-person, five-month agency-style planLeast founder time day-to-day≈5× overspend; you don't own the knowledge; and a team unfamiliar with the device-deletes-on-ACK and evidence-admissibility traps will get exactly the subtle, load-bearing things wrong

Why handing the whole thing off is the riskiest option here

For most products, "give it to a competent dev team" is the safe choice. This product is the exception, and the reason is specific: its value lives in a handful of subtle correctness properties — acknowledge only after durable write, never merge ecu with estimated, resolve every record against the assignment true at its own timestamp, produce evidence that survives a dispute. A generalist team that has never shipped telematics will treat these as ordinary CRUD, build something that demos beautifully, and leave you with a billing number you can't defend. You would pay five times as much to lose control of the one thing that makes the product sellable. The correctness traps are not where an outside team adds value; they are where an outside team costs you the most.

Recommendation — a hybrid. Self-build the thin vertical slice with Claude to de-risk and learn (P0–P2), leaning on Claude for the modules it leads (3, 4, 6, 7). Bring in one experienced backend engineer — hire or contract — to own the spine's durability and the ledger/evidence module (5). Keep the field coordinator and auto-electrician part-time/contract. Do not hand the whole build to a generalist dev team. This is path A and B fused: Claude for breadth and speed, one strong human for the dangerous parts, the knowledge staying with you. It is the best balance of cost, control, and — the thing that actually matters here — correctness you can stand behind when a customer disputes an invoice.

11Production-grade non-negotiables

"Production grade" for a telematics billing system is not a longer feature list — it is a short list of properties that, if any one is missing, quietly makes the product unsellable. Treat this as the definition-of-done gate for the pilot. Every item is a "must," not a "should."

AreaNon-negotiableWhy it bites
DurabilityACK only after the record is durably stored; ingest is idempotent (dedupe on device + sequence); back-pressure never drops data silentlyThe device deletes its only copy on ACK — a premature ACK is permanent, invisible data loss
Evidence integrityUtilisation records are immutable once sealed; hash-chained or manifested; 7-year retention; ecu and estimated never merged; NULL ≠ zeroA record you can't prove is unchanged is a record a customer can dispute away
TenancyRow-level security enforced and tested; every record resolved against the assignment true at its own timestampOne tenant seeing another's machines — or billing the wrong owner for a period — is a trust-ending bug
SecurityTLS from the device (firmware 03.27.02+); secrets managed, not in code; least-privilege access; private APN / mutual TLS consideredLocation and utilisation data is commercially sensitive; a device is an untrusted edge until proven otherwise
ObservabilityLive metrics: records/sec, buffer depth, CRC-fail & reject rate, per-device last-seen; alert on device silenceA device that stops reporting is lost revenue and a blind spot — you must notice within minutes, not at invoice time
TestingA device-traffic simulator (replay real frames); a kill-the-server-mid-stream durability test; an RLS isolation test; engine-hours reconciliation vs the machine meterThese four tests exercise the exact properties that make or break billing trust; nothing else proves them
Backups & DRAutomated backups from day one; a tested restore; documented RTO / RPOAn untested backup is not a backup — and here the data being backed up is the billing evidence
SLOsDefined ingestion availability, a near-zero acceptable data-loss window, and a dashboard-freshness targetWithout a number, "is it working?" has no answer — and customers will ask

If you cut anything, do not cut these two: the durability discipline (module 1) and the tested restore (backups). Everything else degrades gracefully — a missing dashboard state is ugly, a missing alert is inconvenient. But a premature ACK or an unrestorable backup destroys billing evidence permanently and silently, and you will only discover it when a customer disputes an invoice you can no longer defend. Those two are the floor.

12Recommendation & the first 30 days

Pulling it together: build with Claude plus one experienced backend engineer and a part-time field coordinator; run Traccar in front of a single Postgres/PostGIS (matching your MVP stack via Supabase) and migrate to a custom gateway only when engine hours start billing externally; and above all, buy the first six weeks, not the five months — prove one thin vertical slice end-to-end before you scale the width. Do not hand the whole thing to a generalist dev team; the correctness traps are exactly what an unfamiliar team gets wrong, at five times the cost.
